Session 3 · Potential outcomes

Book chapter 4

Read before the meeting: Chapter 4 — Potential Outcomes Causal Model. Data: data/yule.dta, data/thornton_hiv.dta, data/ri.dta. Code files: code/session03.py · code/session03.R · Slides

Learning goals

Participants should be able to write the simple difference in outcomes (SDO) as the average treatment effect (ATE) plus selection bias plus heterogeneous-treatment-effect bias, explain why physical randomization zeroes out the last two terms, and run a Fisher randomization-inference test by hand: state the sharp null, enumerate (or sample) the assignments, and compute an exact \(p\)-value.

Key ideas

Potential outcomes. Each unit \(i\) has \(Y_i^1\) (outcome if treated) and \(Y_i^0\) (if not). The individual treatment effect is \(\delta_i = Y_i^1 - Y_i^0\). We observe only \(Y_i = D_i Y_i^1 + (1 - D_i) Y_i^0\) — the switching equation. The missing potential outcome is the fundamental problem of causal inference; every design in this book is a strategy for filling it in credibly.

In plain English

For any one person there are two possible futures — with the treatment and without — and you only get to watch one. The effect on that person is the gap between the two futures, which you can never see directly. Every method in this book is a trick for estimating the future you didn’t get to watch, using other people who did.

Estimands. The average treatment effect, \(ATE = E[\delta]\), averages over everyone. The average treatment effect on the treated, \(ATT = E[\delta \mid D = 1]\), averages over the units that actually got treated. The average treatment effect on the untreated, \(ATU = E[\delta \mid D = 0]\), averages over those that did not. They differ whenever effects are heterogeneous and treatment is selected on them.

In plain English

ATE: “How much would it help on average if we gave it to everyone?” ATT: “How much did it help the people who actually got it?” ATU: “How much would it have helped the people who didn’t?” These can be very different numbers — a job-training program might help the people who sign up (ATT) a lot more than it would help a random person (ATE).

The decomposition. The simple difference in outcomes (SDO) — the treated group’s average minus the untreated group’s — is \[E[Y \mid D=1] - E[Y \mid D=0] = ATE + \underbrace{E[Y^0 \mid D=1] - E[Y^0 \mid D=0]}_{\text{selection bias}} + \underbrace{(1-\pi)(ATT - ATU)}_{\text{heterogeneous-effect (HTE) bias}}.\] Selection bias: treated units would have done differently even untreated. HTE bias: treated units gain differently from treatment. Randomization makes \(D\) independent of \((Y^0, Y^1)\), so both bias terms are zero and the SDO is the ATE.

In plain English

Comparing treated and untreated averages gives you the true effect plus two kinds of junk. Junk #1 (selection bias): the treated group would have done differently anyway — healthier people take the gym membership. Junk #2 (heterogeneous-effect bias): the treatment helps the treated group by a different amount than it would help everyone else. A coin flip deciding who gets treated makes both junk terms average out to zero.

Randomization inference (RI). Fisher’s sharp null, \(H_0: \delta_i = 0\) for every \(i\), fills in every missing potential outcome (they equal the observed ones). Under it, you can recompute the test statistic for every possible assignment and get an exact \(p\)-value with no distributional assumptions and no appeal to large samples. It is the right tool for small experiments and a useful stress test for any design.

In plain English

Ask: “If the treatment did absolutely nothing to anyone, how often would a random reshuffle of who got treated produce a gap as big as the one I found?” You answer by actually doing the reshuffles — hundreds or thousands of them — and counting. If big gaps show up in 40% of reshuffles, your result is unremarkable; if they show up in 0.1%, it is not.

Step 3 — Fisher’s exact \(p\)-value on the 8-unit toy data

ri.dta has four treated and four control units. The sharp null says every unit’s \(Y^1 = Y^0 = Y\), so we can recompute the difference in means for all \(\binom{8}{4} = 70\) assignments.

  • Python
  • R
import itertools

ri = pd.read_stata("data/ri.dta")
y = ri["y"].to_numpy()
d = ri["d"].to_numpy()
obs = y[d == 1].mean() - y[d == 0].mean()

stats = []
for treated in itertools.combinations(range(8), 4):
    mask = np.zeros(8, dtype=bool); mask[list(treated)] = True
    stats.append(y[mask].mean() - y[~mask].mean())
stats = np.array(stats)

p = (np.abs(stats) >= abs(obs)).mean()
print(f"observed diff = {obs:.2f}, exact two-sided p = {p:.3f} over {len(stats)} assignments")
observed diff = 1.00, exact two-sided p = 0.857 over 70 assignments
ri <- read_dta("data/ri.dta")
y <- ri$y; d <- ri$d
obs <- mean(y[d == 1]) - mean(y[d == 0])

combos <- combn(8, 4)
stats <- apply(combos, 2, function(idx) mean(y[idx]) - mean(y[-idx]))
p <- mean(abs(stats) >= abs(obs))
c(observed = obs, p_value = p, n_assignments = ncol(combos))
     observed       p_value n_assignments 
    1.0000000     0.8571429    70.0000000

Observed difference = 1.0; 60 of 70 assignments give a difference at least that large in absolute value, so \(p ≈\) 0.86. With eight units you learn almost nothing, and the exact test says so without pretending otherwise.

Step 4 — Randomization inference on Thornton

With 4,820 people there are too many assignments to enumerate; sample 1,000 random permutations of the treatment vector.

  • Python
  • R
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
got = hiv["got"].to_numpy(); anyv = hiv["any"].to_numpy()
obs = got[anyv == 1].mean() - got[anyv == 0].mean()

perm = np.empty(1000)
for i in range(1000):
    a = rng.permutation(anyv)
    perm[i] = got[a == 1].mean() - got[a == 0].mean()

print("observed:", round(obs, 3))
observed: 0.451
print("largest |placebo| diff:", np.abs(perm).max().round(3))
largest |placebo| diff: 0.068
print("p-value:", (np.abs(perm) >= abs(obs)).mean())
p-value: 0.0

The largest placebo difference is around 0.07; the observed 0.45 is more than six times further out. $p = $ 0 at 1,000 draws. The histogram with the red line is the slide to keep: it is the sampling distribution under the sharp null.

Exercises

  1. Decompose the bias. Build a population of 10,000 units with \(Y^0 \sim N(10, 2)\) and \(\delta_i \sim N(2, 1)\). Let units select into treatment when \(Y^1 - Y^0 > 2.3\) (they take it if it helps them more than average). Compute the true ATE, ATT, ATU, the SDO, and verify the decomposition formula numerically term by term.
  2. RI with a different statistic. Redo Step 3 using the difference in medians and then the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic. Do the \(p\)-values change? When would you want a statistic other than the mean difference?
  3. Thornton heterogeneity. The incentive amount tinc varied. Plot the share who got results by incentive amount (bin it) and test whether any positive incentive versus none explains most of the effect. What does this say about the ATE being an average over very different \(\delta_i\)?

Solutions

Solutions (click to expand)

1.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0); N = 10_000
y0 = rng.normal(10, 2, N); delta = rng.normal(2, 1, N); y1 = y0 + delta
D = (delta > 2.3).astype(int); Y = np.where(D == 1, y1, y0)
ATE = delta.mean(); ATT = delta[D==1].mean(); ATU = delta[D==0].mean()
SDO = Y[D==1].mean() - Y[D==0].mean()
sel = y0[D==1].mean() - y0[D==0].mean()
pi = D.mean()
print(SDO, ATE + sel + (1-pi)*(ATT-ATU))

Selection bias is ≈ 0 here (selection is on \(\delta\), not on \(Y^0\)), but HTE bias is large: ATT ≈ 3.0 vs ATU ≈ 1.4, so the SDO (≈ 3.0) overstates the ATE (= 2.0) by about 1.

2. The median-difference \(p\)-value on 8 units is similar (≈ 0.9). KS is more sensitive to distributional differences beyond location; use it when treatment might change spread or shape rather than the mean.

3. Collection rates jump from 34% at zero incentive to about 67% for the smallest positive amounts (under half a dollar), then rise to about 85% for amounts over a dollar. Most of the 0.45 ATE is the extensive margin; individual \(\delta_i\) clearly differ (for people who would have collected anyway, \(\delta_i = 0\)).

Facilitator notes

Facilitator notes (click to expand)

Spend the time on the decomposition. Write it on the board with the three terms labeled and keep pointing at it for the rest of the series; every later design is a story about which term it kills.

Misconception: “randomization makes individuals comparable.” It makes groups comparable in expectation. Use Step 3 to show that in a tiny sample randomization has done very little.

Misconception: RI is a bootstrap. The bootstrap resamples the data to approximate sampling variation; RI re-randomizes assignment holding the data fixed. Different null, different question.

If running short. Do Step 3 and skip Step 4; Step 4 is mostly a scaling exercise.

Discussion prompts. (a) Yule had a control for the elderly share and population growth; why wasn’t that enough? (b) In Thornton, why cluster by village?

Bridge to next session. Without randomization, selection bias is the enemy. Matching assumes we can see everything that drives selection and condition on it — the backdoor criterion from Session 2 meets the decomposition from today.
