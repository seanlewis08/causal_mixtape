Session 9 · Synthetic control, and which design when

Book chapters 10 and 11

Read before the meeting: Chapter 10 — Synthetic Control and Chapter 11 — Conclusion. Data: data/texas.dta (Cunningham & Kang 2019). Code files: code/session09.py · code/session09.R · Slides

Learning goals

Participants should be able to explain the synthetic-control estimator as a constrained weighted average of donor units chosen to match the treated unit’s pre-period, fit one and read the weights, run placebo-in-space inference and compute the treated unit’s rank, and — the point of the final session — look at a new causal question and say which of the book’s designs fits it and why.

Key ideas

The comparative case study. One treated aggregate unit (a state, a country), a handful of candidate controls, and a long pre-period. Difference-in-differences (DiD) with one treated unit has no credible standard error and picks the control arbitrarily. Synthetic control replaces the choice with an algorithm: find weights \(w_j \geq 0\), \(\sum w_j = 1\), over donor units \(j\) that make the weighted average of donors match the treated unit’s pre-treatment outcomes and predictors as closely as possible. The post-period gap between the treated unit and its synthetic version is the estimated effect.

In plain English

Texas did something; no other single state is a fair comparison. So build a “fake Texas” by blending other states — 56% Florida, 27% New York, 17% Illinois — with the blend chosen so that fake Texas tracked real Texas closely for the nine years before the policy. If the two then split apart after the policy, the gap is your estimate.

Why the constraints matter. Non-negativity and summing to one keep the synthetic unit inside the convex hull of the donors: no extrapolation, and the weights are interpretable (“Texas is 56% Florida, 27% New York…”). A poor pre-period fit means the method has nothing to say; report the pre-period root mean squared prediction error (RMSPE).

In plain English

The weights have to be positive and add up to 100%, which means fake Texas is a genuine blend — not “three Californias minus two Floridas,” which would be numerically fine and meaningless. And if the blend never tracked real Texas well before the policy, the comparison afterwards is worthless; the pre-period fit number is the first thing to report.

Inference by placebo. Reassign treatment to each donor in turn, fit its synthetic control, and compute its post/pre RMSPE ratio. If the treated unit’s ratio is extreme relative to the placebo distribution, the effect is unlikely to be luck. The “p-value” is the treated unit’s rank divided by the number of units.

In plain English

Pretend Florida was the treated state and build a fake Florida; see how big a post-period gap you get by chance. Do that for all fifty states. If real Texas’s gap (relative to how well its fake tracked it beforehand) is the biggest of the fifty-one, the odds of that happening by luck are about one in fifty-one.

The Texas question. Texas massively expanded prison capacity in 1993–1994. Black male incarceration roughly doubled over the following years. Did the building cause the filling, relative to a synthetic Texas built from other states?

What the book wants you to leave with. Every chapter answered the same question: where does the exogenous variation come from? Randomization (Ch. 4), observable selection you can model (Ch. 5), a threshold (Ch. 6), an instrument (Ch. 7), within-unit change (Ch. 8), a policy difference across groups and time (Ch. 9), a weighted comparison unit (Ch. 10). The method follows the source of variation, never the reverse.

In plain English

Every design in this book is an answer to one question: what decided who got the treatment, and was it anything like a coin flip? An actual coin flip, a cutoff rule, a quirk of geography, a change over time, a policy that hit some places and not others. Find that first; the method follows from it. If you can’t find it, no method will manufacture it.

Walkthrough

Step 1 — Fit the synthetic Texas

Predictors follow the book: poverty and income averaged over the pre-period, plus the Black male prison population in selected years, alcohol consumption, AIDS incidence, the Black population share and the 15–19 share.

  • R
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from pysyncon import Dataprep, Synth        # pip install pysyncon

tx = pd.read_stata("data/texas.dta", convert_categoricals=False)
names = tx.drop_duplicates("statefip").set_index("statefip")["state"]
TEXAS = 48
donors = [s for s in tx.statefip.unique() if s != TEXAS]

dp = Dataprep(
    foo=tx, dependent="bmprison", unit_variable="statefip", time_variable="year",
    treatment_identifier=TEXAS, controls_identifier=donors,
    predictors=["poverty", "income"], predictors_op="mean", time_predictors_prior=range(1985, 1994),
    special_predictors=[
        ("bmprison", range(1988, 1989), "mean"), ("bmprison", range(1990, 1991), "mean"),
        ("bmprison", range(1991, 1992), "mean"), ("bmprison", range(1992, 1993), "mean"),
        ("alcohol", range(1990, 1991), "mean"),  ("aidscapita", range(1990, 1992), "mean"),
        ("black", range(1990, 1992), "mean"),    ("perc1519", range(1990, 1991), "mean"),
    ],
    time_optimize_ssr=range(1985, 1994),
)
synth = Synth()
synth.fit(dataprep=dp, optim_method="BFGS", optim_initial="ols")   # ~20 s; the default Nelder-Mead can take many minutes

Step 3 — Placebo-in-space inference

  • Python
  • R
def fit_state(st):
    d = Dataprep(foo=tx, dependent="bmprison", unit_variable="statefip", time_variable="year",
                 treatment_identifier=st, controls_identifier=[s for s in tx.statefip.unique() if s != st],
                 predictors=["poverty", "income"], predictors_op="mean", time_predictors_prior=range(1985, 1994),
                 special_predictors=dp.special_predictors, time_optimize_ssr=range(1985, 1994))
    s = Synth(); s.fit(dataprep=d, optim_method="BFGS", optim_initial="ols")
    Z0, Z1 = d.make_outcome_mats(time_period=range(1985, 2001)); g = Z1 - Z0 @ s.W
    pre  = np.sqrt((g.loc[1985:1993] ** 2).mean())
    post = np.sqrt((g.loc[1994:2000] ** 2).mean())
    return pd.Series({"pre_rmspe": pre, "post_rmspe": post, "ratio": post / pre})

# This loop fits 51 synthetic controls and takes several minutes.
placebo = pd.DataFrame({names[st]: fit_state(st) for st in tx.statefip.unique()}).T
placebo = placebo.sort_values("ratio", ascending=False)
print(placebo.head(10).round(1))
rank = int((placebo.index == "Texas").argmax()) + 1
print(f"Texas rank: {rank} of {len(placebo)}  ->  p = {rank/len(placebo):.3f}")

Texas has the largest post/pre RMSPE ratio of all 51 units: ≈ 25.9, against 20.4 for the runner-up (Illinois) and single digits for most states. The rank-based \(p = 1/51 ≈\) 0.02. The placebo-gap plot shows Texas’s line leaving the band of 50 grey placebo lines after 1993. The book’s convention is to drop placebos whose pre-period RMSPE is more than some multiple of the treated unit’s; here only one state exceeds twice Texas’s, so the result does not depend on it.

Step 4 — Wrap-up: which design, when

Put three fresh questions to the room and make the group pick a design and name its key assumption and its first diagnostic. Suggested questions:

  1. Did a city’s new minimum-wage ordinance reduce restaurant employment? — difference-in-differences or synthetic control against comparable cities; parallel trends / pre-period fit; event-study leads or placebo-in-space.
  2. Does an extra year of schooling raise earnings for students who barely pass a school-entry test? — regression discontinuity (RDD) on the test score; continuity at the cutoff; density test and covariate smoothness.
  3. Do defendants assigned public defenders get longer sentences than those with private counsel? — Selection on unobservables is severe; an instrumental variable (IV) such as random assignment of public-defender availability (judge-leniency style); relevance and exclusion; first-stage F and a complier description.

Then the decision tree, in words. If treatment was randomized, compare means and use randomization inference. If selection is on observables you can defend with a DAG, match or weight, and check overlap. If treatment switches at a threshold, RD. If something exogenous shifts treatment but not the outcome, IV, and say whose effect you are estimating. If you observe units before and after a change, fixed effects or DiD, with an event study to show the pre-period. If you have one treated aggregate and a long pre-period, synthetic control with placebo inference. If none of those apply, the honest answer is that the data cannot answer the causal question, and that is a finding.

Exercises

  1. Leave-one-out. Drop Florida (the largest-weight donor) from the donor pool and refit the synthetic Texas. How much do the weights and the post-period gap change? Then also drop the state with the next-largest weight. What does sensitivity to a single donor tell you?
  2. Placebo-in-time. Pretend treatment happened in 1989 and fit the synthetic control using only 1985–1988 to choose weights. Is there a spurious “effect” after 1989? Why is this a useful check in addition to placebo-in-space?
  3. Design memo. Pick a causal question from your own work. In one page: state the estimand (average effect for everyone, for the treated, for compliers, or local to a cutoff — ATE, ATT, LATE, or RD…), the design you would use, its identifying assumption written as a counterfactual statement, two diagnostics you would show, and the one thing that would make you abandon the design.

Solutions

Solutions (click to expand)

1. Without Florida the weight shifts to Illinois (0.44), New York (0.35) and California (0.21); the post-period gap is ≈ 23,600 and the pre-RMSPE rises only slightly (≈ 1,050). Dropping New York as well leaves Illinois 0.56 / California 0.44, a gap of ≈ 23,000 and pre-RMSPE ≈ 1,250. The result is not an artifact of any one donor. Large sensitivity to one donor would mean the synthetic unit was really a single comparison state, and you would be back to a one-control DiD.

2. With a 1989 pseudo-treatment, the synthetic and actual series continue to track through 1993, with no gap that resembles the post-1993 one. Placebo-in-time guards against the pre-period fit being achieved by overfitting a short window; placebo-in-space guards against the post-period gap being within the range of ordinary noise.

3. Discussed in the meeting. A good memo names the counterfactual explicitly (“absent the ordinance, employment in city A would have followed the trend in cities B–D”).

Facilitator notes

Facilitator notes (click to expand)

Run Step 3 before the meeting. The 51-fit placebo loop takes several minutes in Python; have the results table ready and only re-run the Texas fit live. tidysynth with generate_placebos = TRUE does the loop inside generate_weights().

Misconception: the weights are “the” comparison states. They are the solution to an optimization with the predictors you chose. Change the predictors and the weights move (Step 1’s expect box shows this between implementations). What should not move much is the post-period gap.

Misconception: a pre-period fit that looks good is good enough. Insist on the pre-RMSPE number and the placebo-in-time check. Synthetic control with a short or badly fitted pre-period is a very elaborate way of picking a control state by hand.

Wrap-up block. Give it the full 15 minutes. It is the only time in the series the group practices choosing a design rather than executing one, and it is the skill the book is actually teaching.
