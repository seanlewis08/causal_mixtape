Session 6 · Instrumental variables

Book chapter 7

Read before the meeting: Chapter 7 — Instrumental Variables. Data: data/card.dta; judge_fe.dta (58 MB, downloaded from the book’s repo — see Step 4). Code files: code/session06.py · code/session06.R · Slides

Learning goals

Participants should be able to state the three instrumental-variable (IV) conditions (relevance, exclusion, independence) and the fourth that makes the estimand interpretable (monotonicity), compute a Wald / two-stage least squares (2SLS) estimate and explain it as reduced form divided by first stage, diagnose weak instruments with the first-stage F, and explain why the IV estimand under heterogeneous effects is a local average treatment effect (LATE) for compliers — and who the compliers are in the judge-leniency design.

Key ideas

The problem IV solves. \(Y = \alpha + \delta D + u\) with \(\mathrm{Cov}(D, u) \neq 0\). Regression cannot recover \(\delta\). But if some instrumental variable (IV) \(Z\) moves \(D\) and is otherwise unrelated to \(Y\), then the only reason \(Y\) moves with \(Z\) is through \(D\), so \[\delta_{IV} = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(Z, Y)}{\mathrm{Cov}(Z, D)} = \frac{\text{reduced form}}{\text{first stage}}.\]

In plain English

You can’t trust the raw relationship between schooling and wages because ambitious people get more of both. But suppose some people happened to grow up near a college. That nudges them toward more schooling, and — if it has no other effect on wages — any wage difference between near-college and far-from-college people must have come through schooling. Divide the wage gap by the schooling gap and you have the effect of schooling.

The conditions. Relevance: \(Z\) predicts \(D\) (testable; look at the first-stage F). Exclusion: \(Z\) affects \(Y\) only through \(D\) (untestable; argued from the DAG). Independence: \(Z\) is as good as random with respect to \(u\) (untestable). Philip Wright’s 1928 appendix is the origin story: identify a demand curve with a supply shifter (weather) that does not enter demand.

In plain English

An instrument has one job and one restriction. The job: it has to actually move the treatment (you can check this). The restriction: it must not touch the outcome any other way, and it must not itself be chosen by people for reasons related to the outcome (you cannot check either; you have to argue them). Most IV debates are about the restriction.

Two-stage least squares (2SLS). Regress \(D\) on \(Z\) and covariates, take the fitted values \(\hat{D}\), regress \(Y\) on \(\hat{D}\) and the same covariates. The second-stage standard errors must account for the first stage; use a proper IV routine, not two lm calls.

Weak instruments. When the first stage is weak, 2SLS is biased toward OLS and its sampling distribution is badly behaved. The rule of thumb is first-stage F above 10 for one endogenous regressor; the modern advice is to report it, prefer just-identified models, and be nervous below 20.

In plain English

If the instrument barely nudges the treatment, you are dividing a small number by another small number, and the answer swings wildly — and, worse, tends to drift back toward the naive estimate you were trying to avoid. The first-stage F-statistic measures how hard the nudge is; under 10 is a warning light.

Local average treatment effect (LATE). With heterogeneous effects, IV identifies the average effect for compliers — units whose treatment is moved by the instrument — under monotonicity (no defiers). Different instruments identify different LATEs for the same treatment. The judge-leniency design makes this vivid: the compliers are defendants who would be detained by a strict judge and released by a lenient one.

In plain English

IV only tells you about the people the instrument actually moved. Growing up near a college changes the plans of some teenagers and not others; the IV estimate is the return to schooling for those teenagers, not for everyone. A different instrument moves different people and can honestly give a different answer. “Local” means “for the movers.”

Step 1 — Card (1995): OLS returns to schooling

  • R

Step 4 — Judge leniency as an instrument (Stevenson 2018)

Defendants are quasi-randomly assigned to magistrates who differ in how often they set bail the defendant cannot pay. Judge indicators instrument for pretrial detention (jail3), and the outcome is whether the defendant is found guilty.

OLS: detention raises the probability of a guilty finding by ≈ 0.035 (3.5 points). 2SLS: ≈ 0.16 (SE ≈ 0.07) — more than four times larger. The first-stage F is in the hundreds. Interpretation: for defendants whose detention depends on which judge they drew, being detained raises conviction by 16 points — the pressure to plead.

Exercises

  1. Just-identified vs over-identified. Re-estimate Card’s model using nearc2 (near a two-year college) as a second instrument alongside nearc4. Report the 2SLS estimate, the first-stage F, and (in R, summary(..., diagnostics = TRUE); in Python, iv.sargan) the Sargan over-identification test. What does a rejection mean and why is it not a test of the exclusion restriction?
  2. Weak-instrument simulation. Simulate \(Y = D + u\), \(D = \pi Z + v\) with \(\mathrm{Corr}(u, v) = 0.8\), \(n = 500\). For \(\pi \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5\}\), run 1,000 replications and record OLS, 2SLS, and the first-stage F. Plot the sampling distribution of 2SLS for each \(\pi\). At what F does 2SLS start to look well-behaved?
  3. Who are the compliers? Using Card, compute the share of compliers implied by the first stage (it is the first-stage coefficient when \(D\) is binary; here educ is continuous, so think of a binary version: educ >= 13). Then compare the mean of black and south among compliers to the full sample using the Abadie (2003) kappa-weighting trick or the simpler ratio-of-first-stages method from the book. Are compliers representative?

Solutions

Solutions (click to expand)

1. With both instruments the estimate rises to ≈ 0.16 (SE ≈ 0.05) and the first-stage F is ≈ 19; the Sargan statistic is 3.6 on 1 df, \(p ≈ 0.06\) — borderline. A rejection would say the two instruments imply different LATEs, which could be heterogeneity rather than invalidity; the test can never validate the exclusion restriction of either instrument on its own.

2. OLS is ≈ 1.8 regardless of \(\pi\) (the endogeneity bias). At \(\pi = 0.05\) the median first-stage F is ≈ 1 and the 2SLS median is ≈ 1.3 with an interquartile range of about 0.8–1.7, pulled toward OLS; at \(\pi = 0.1\) (F ≈ 5) the median is right but the IQR is still 0.6–1.3; by \(\pi = 0.2\) (F ≈ 20) it is tight around 1 (IQR 0.8–1.1). The F ≈ 10 threshold is where the worst behavior fades, not where it ends.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def sim(pi, n=500):
    z = rng.normal(size=n); uv = rng.multivariate_normal([0,0], [[1,.8],[.8,1]], n)
    d = pi*z + uv[:,1]; y = d + uv[:,0]
    df = pd.DataFrame(dict(y=y, d=d, z=z))
    fs = smf.ols("d ~ z", df).fit()
    return smf.ols("y ~ d", df).fit().params["d"], \
           IV2SLS.from_formula("y ~ 1 + [d ~ z]", df).fit().params["d"], fs.fvalue

3. With D = educ >= 13, the first stage on nearc4 is about 0.06, i.e. roughly 6% of the sample are compliers. Using the ratio-of-first-stages method (\(P(x=1 \mid \text{complier}) / P(x=1) = \text{FS}_{x=1} / \text{FS}\)), compliers are far less likely to be Black (ratio ≈ 0.2) and less likely to be Southern (ratio ≈ 0.6) than the full sample. The LATE is for a population that looks quite different from the average respondent — one more reason not to call it “the” return to schooling.

Facilitator notes

Facilitator notes (click to expand)

Do Step 2 by hand before Step 3. The Wald ratio is the whole idea; 2SLS is bookkeeping. If people see 0.04 / 0.33 ≈ 0.12 with their own eyes, the software output stops being magic.

Misconception: a significant first stage proves the instrument is valid. It proves relevance only. Exclusion and independence come from the argument, never from the data.

Misconception: IV estimates “the” effect. Keep saying LATE. Card’s compliers and Stevenson’s compliers are different people with different effects; neither is the ATE.

On the judge data. It is 58 MB; have people download it before the meeting. If the network is bad, skip Step 4 and talk through the design on the slides — the numbers are in the expect box.

On collinearity. Stata and R silently drop collinear controls; linearmodels raises an error. The trimmed Python control list is the same model.

Discussion prompts. (a) Why might college proximity violate exclusion? (b) In the judge design, what is monotonicity, and is it plausible that every judge is weakly stricter than another for every defendant?

Bridge to next session. IV finds exogenous variation in a cross-section. Panel data gets it from within-unit changes over time, assuming the unobservables that matter are fixed.
